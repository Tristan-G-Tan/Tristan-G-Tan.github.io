Science Practices (Myers' Unit 0)

research design/methodology2.A (A) Identify the research method used in the study.
Experimental methodology2.B involves the use of independent variable(s) and random assignment to groups.
independent variable Whatever is being manipulated in the experiment.
dependent variable Whatever is being measured in the experiment.
confounding variable Variables that have unwanted influence on the outcome of an experiment; anything that differs between the control group and the experimental group besides the independent variable.
random assignment Assigning participants to experimental and control groups by chance, thus minimizing preexisting differences (confounding variables) between the different groups. This is done to infer causation (NOT random sampling; that's for generalizability).
control group and placebo the group not exposed to the treatment; contrasts with the experimental group and serves as a comparison for evaluating the effect of the treatment.
placebo a treatment with an inactive substance
placebo effect The placebo effect is the difference between the average response to a placebo and the average response to no treatment.
single-blind In a single-blind, also called single-masked, experiment, participants do not know which treatment they are receiving, but members of the research team who interact with them know which treatment each participant is receiving, or vice versa.
double-blind In a double-blind, also called double-masked, experiment, neither the participants nor the members of the research team who interact with them know which treatment each participant is receiving.
Non-experimental methodologies2.C An observational (cf. experimental) study is a study where treatments are not imposed. The researcher records the values of the variables of interest in order to explore an investigative question of interest.
case study One individual or group is studied in depth in the hope of revealing universal principles.
correlation Determining a relationship between two variables in which change in one variable are reflected in the changes in the other variable.
correlation does not equal causation causation requires experiments and random assignment; demonstrated by:
directionality problem Variable A could cause Variable B, Variable B could cause Variable A (reverse causation), or both could influence each other.
third variable problem A situation where a third variable is responsible for the observed correlation between two other variables.
correlation coefficient3.C

The correlation coefficient, r, summarizes the strength and direction of the linear association between two quantitative variables. The correlation coefficient r is unit-free and always between −1 and 1, inclusive.

A negative correlation coefficient value indicates a negative association, and a positive correlation coefficient value indicates a positive association.

The strength of the linear association is determined by how close the correlation coefficient is to −1 or 1. A value of r = 0 indicates that there is no linear association. A value of r = -1 or r =1 indicates that there is a perfect linear association.

meta-analysis synthesizes data from multiple independent studies (often on the same research question or topic) to generate a single, more precise, and comprehensive conclusion; uses quantitative techniques (compare literature review, which is qualitative)
naturalistic observation Subjects are observed in their natural environment.
survey (in MCQs but not directly in FRQ AAQs) an observational study in which the data are collected from humans using a standard set of questions.
cross-sectional study research that compares people of different ages at the same point in time.
longitudinal study research that follows and retests the same people over time.
ethical guidelines2.D
institutional review An official committee reviews research plans before the study starts, to judge if the study is ethical for human or animal subjects.
informed consent and/or informed assent
consent adult participants fully understand study risks, benefits, tasks, and can freely choose to join or quit.
assent children/teens show willingness to take part, while their guardians give formal permission.
protect participants from harm Researchers must take steps to avoid physical, mental, or emotional injury to participants
participant information confidential and/or anonymous
confidentiality Researchers know participants' identities, but promise not to reveal their personal information to outsiders.
anonymity No one (even researchers) can link data back to individual participants, no names are collected.
deception Researchers hide or lie about the true study purpose, only allowed when no better way exists and harm is minimal.
research confederates people pretending to be regular participants
debriefing At the end of research, researchers fully explain the true purpose of the study, especially if deception was used. They answer participants' questions and ease any negative feelings.

Descriptive statistics3.B
Shape
skewness

AP Statistics CED EK 1.6.A.2: The shape of the distribution of one quantitative variable is skewed to the right (positively skewed) if the right tail (toward larger values) is longer than the left. The shape of the distribution is skewed to the left (negatively skewed) if the left tail (toward smaller values) is longer than the right.

AP Statistics CED EK 1.8.B.1: If a distribution is relatively symmetric, then the values of the mean and median are relatively close to each other. If a distribution is skewed right, then the value of the mean is usually larger than the median. If the distribution is skewed left, then the value of the mean is usually smaller than the median.

modality AP Statistics CED EK 1.6.A.3 Distributions with two prominent peaks are called bimodal.
normal distribution curve

Many continuous random variables are well modeled by a normal distribution.

A normal distribution can be described as a continuous, unimodal, bell-shaped, and symmetric curve.

The normal distribution, or the normal curve, is identified by two parameters, the mean, µ, and the standard deviation, σ . The smaller the standard deviation, the taller and more concentrated the normal curve is around its mean. The larger the standard deviation, the shorter and less concentrated the normal curve is around its mean.

The empirical rule can be used to estimate the area of a region under the graph of the normal distribution curve. For a normal distribution, approximately 68% of observations are within 1 standard deviation of the mean, approximately 95% of observations are within 2 standard deviations of the mean, and approximately 99.7% of observations are within 3 standard deviations of the mean. This is called the empirical rule, or the 68–95–99.7 rule.

Easily confused terms
random sampling vs random assignment

The former is for generalizability. The latter is for causation (rather than just correlation).

AP Statistics CED EK 1.10.E.2: When observational units, or experimental units, in a sample are randomly selected from a population, it is appropriate to make generalizations about the entire population of individuals from which the sample was selected.

AP Statistics CED EK 1.10.E.4: When observational units, or experimental units, in a sample are not randomly selected from a population, it is appropriate to make generalizations only about a population of individuals that are similar to those used in the study.

AP Statistics CED EK 1.13.A.7: The purpose of random assignment is to create treatment groups that are as similar as possible with respect to extraneous sources of variation. If random assignment is successful, the respective distributions of each extraneous variable will be approximately the same for all the treatment groups.

AP Statistics CED EK 1.13.D.1: Using random assignment of treatments to experimental units allows for cause-and-effect conclusions between the explanatory and the response variables because the potential for confounding variables is reduced.

random vs representative sample
extraneous vs confounding variable

The latter is a subset of the former. Extraneous variables might only affect the dependent variable, or they might be random noise. It becomes confounding when it links to both the independent and dependent variables

AP Statistics CED EK 1.13.A.6: An extraneous source of variation, also referred to as an extraneous variable, is a variable that is known (or believed) to affect the response but is not an explanatory variable being studied.

AP Statistics CED EK 1.10.D.5: A confounding variable in an observational study provides an alternative explanation for the observed relationship between the explanatory and response variables determined in the study, thereby reducing the possibility of concluding a causal relationship between the explanatory and response variables of interest. To be a confounding variable, a variable must be associated with both the explanatory variable and the response variable.

AP Statistics CED EK 1.13.A.8: A confounding variable in an experiment is a variable that is related to the explanatory variable in such a way that it is difficult to determine which variable, explanatory or confounding, is influencing the change in the response variable. However, in a well-designed experiment, the potential for confounding variables is reduced.

drug tolerance, addiction, dependence, and withdrawal1.3.C.3 Tolerance is the diminished effect with the same dose. Addiction is craving. Dependence (not in the CED) is reliance. Withdrawal comes last (you have to quit first to see the withdrawal symptoms).
resting potential, action potential, threshold (potential), absolute threshold, and difference threshold (just-noticeable difference, JND)

The first 3 are about neural transmission1.3.B.1. Membrane potential (y-axis) is usually at the resting potential, typically -70 millivolts (mV). If it ever increases past the threshold (potential), typically -55 mV, it is now an action potential (the neuron fires) and will keep increasing (known as depolarization) until typically +30 to +40 mV. Then the membrane potential falls (known as repolarization) and, during the refractory period, dips even below the resting potential.

The last 2 are about sensation1.6. The absolute threshold1.6.A.1occurs when a stimulus can be detected at least 50% of the time; if a stimulus is less intense than that, it's subliminal. The just-noticeable difference1.6.A.2(JND, also called the difference threshold) is the smallest detectable change between two stimuli that you can notice 50% of the time, and according to Weber's law, this is a constant proportion rather than a constant difference.

habituation vs sensory adaptation Habituation3.7.A.5is a diminished response, even though sensory detectors still detect it. Sensory adaptation1.6.A.2is when actual sensory receptors become fatigued.
accommodation vs convergence Accommodation is either about eyes1.6.B.2or about schemata2.2.A.2(in which case it's contrasted with assimilation, both being continuous Piagetian processes3.4.A.1). Convergence is a binocular depth cue2.1.B.1(the other being retinal disparity).

Memories

Summary

Dual-Track memory: Explicit=Declarative=Effortful=Conscious; Implicit=Nondeclarative=Automatic=Unconscious.

Working=short-term, just a newer understanding of it. Iconic=visual, echoic=auditory.

explicit/implicit/prospective2.3.A.1 Memories for learned knowledge, events, and experiences are differentiated by how they are processed by, stored in, and retrieved by the brain.
Explicit memory2.3.A.1.i more easily described or explained to others
episodic memory events (time & place), personal experiences
semantic memory language, facts, general knowledge
Implicit memory2.3.A.1.ii more challenging to describe or explain to others
procedural memory for procedures and processes; motor skills, muscle memory (throwing a ball), operant conditioning, classical conditioning, conditioned/unconditioned/primed responses
Prospective memory2.3.A.1.iii related to future actions
working memory model2.3.A.3 examines how our primary memory system—working memory—engages in a dynamic interaction with
central executive boss of working memory: controls our attention (should incoming information go into the phonological loop or the visuospatial sketchpad?), integrates new information with existing long-term memory
phonological loop verbal input: for spoken and written material, repeats it again and again
visuospatial sketchpad visual input: for navigation, stores and processes information in a visual or spatial form
multi-store model2.3.A.4 focuses on the impact of automatic and effortful processing on memory encoding, storage, and retrieval. Information must pass through three interacting systems to be remembered:
sensory memory immediate, very brief recording of sensory information
iconic memory for visual inputs, decays quickly
echoic memory for auditory inputs, decays slightly less quickly
short-term memory lasts 15-30 seconds if you paid attention to sensory information, holds 7 ± 2 (the magic number) items
long-term memory encoded from short-term memory (see2.3.A.3and2.4), can be retrieved later
levels of processing model2.3.A.5 proposes that memory is encoded on three levels from shallowest to deepest:
structural memory encoded from the basic visual/physical qualities of the word/concept, e.g. "baker" has 5 letters, maintenance rehearsal.
phonemic memory encoded from the basic auditory qualities of the word/concept, e.g. "baker" rhymes with "shaker".
semantic memory encoded from the meaning of the word/concept, e.g. a baker makes cakes, cookies, and pastries.
